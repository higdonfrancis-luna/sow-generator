## Delivery economics (for Deloitte and the partner delivery leads)

1. **The hours are planning assumptions.** Every service now names the quantity behind its Small, Typical and Large hours (`sow/estimating_bands.json`), and each card says next to its hours "Hours: a partner planning assumption, not from Adobe." None of those hours comes from delivery actuals or an independent estimate. The file records the estimate owner as "Not yet assigned" and the confidence as low. Still missing per service: the roles, hours per role, complexity and maturity assumptions, and a named estimate owner. Suggested step, from the audit: give Deloitte two realistic client briefs (estate, maturity, scope units, acceptance criteria) and have their delivery leads estimate before they see these numbers, then reconcile. Adam's call (November 1): no outside estimate ("I don't need anyone to check my work"); the hours stay as they are.
2. **The band quantities are ours, and only prompts and markets are inputs.** For example, the Typical site build assumes up to 10 templates, 30 blocks, 3 integrations and 50 pages for 960 hours; the Typical Brand Concierge build assumes up to 3 product lines, 15 sources, 3 journeys and 150 test conversations for 640 hours. They make each estimate testable; they are not Adobe guidance. Only the prompt count and the markets count are number fields; the site build's templates, blocks, integrations and pages, the migration's pages and redirects, and Brand Concierge's products, sources, journeys and test conversations are band descriptions the partner picks on each card. The prompt thresholds (under 200, 200 to 1,000, 1,001 to 3,000, now printed under the field) and market thresholds (one, two or three, four to six) extend the October 29 size definitions; Adobe recommends no prompt count. Bands still step at their edges (1,000 to 1,001 prompts doubles the setup service's hours, about $73,200 more at $150 across the three prompt services), and AI engines and review cadence are not separate inputs.
3. **No cost, margin or overrun model.** The tool shows fees (hours times the partner's rate) and says so. Staffing mix, delivery cost, presales, overruns and margin stay in each partner's own private model. The audit's stress test applies: at a $90 loaded cost, 104 hours billed at $150 is a 40% contribution, and 25% if the work takes 130 hours.
4. **Recurring work carries most of the value.** Standalone Brand Visibility's monthly program is 89% of its displayed total, and Coworker adoption is 384 hours a year. Every monthly service uses one term (12 months until another is entered, on the canvas and in the SOW alike) with no ramp, start month or stop rule. Coworker adoption carries a three-month usage review; the rest needs an agreed backlog, an operating owner and renewal criteria per customer. Still to design, from the audit's practical path: execution, governance and enablement tiers tied to actual needs instead of an automatic twelve-month labor assumption, and expand and stop criteria defined before each pilot starts. Two or three paid pilots that track actual effort, rework, approval delays, costs, customer outcomes and reusable assets would settle it.
5. **What the partner adds beyond the products.** The audit's executive questions: what does the partner do that the product and the customer's team don't, and how does automation improve partner productivity? The monthly review (O01), content improvements (O02) and the Sites Optimizer services (SO1, SO2) now say what the product does on its own (Brand Visibility runs the prompts and lists opportunities; its edge fixes reach only the AI assistants Adobe supports; Sites Optimizer finds and suggests fixes, and deploys them only with its Ultimate package) and what the hours cover. Whether those hours survive, and how fast automation shrinks them, needs the delivery partner's estimate and pilot data. Owner: Adam with the delivery partner.
